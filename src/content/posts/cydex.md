CYDex is a Dex that sits on my desk. It runs on the ESP32-2432S028, the board everyone calls the "Cheap Yellow Display": a 2.8" 320×240 resistive touchscreen, a microSD slot and Wi-Fi, for about $15. It browses all 1025 species, marks the ones I've caught in my own save files, and when I'm not touching it, it turns into a showcase that also tells me when something needs my attention.

Everything runs on the board. There's no server, no cloud and no account.

## The whole catalog, on a microcontroller

The catalog covers nine generations, shown as a list or a 5×2 sprite grid. You can filter by caught or missing, by up to two types, or search by name or number, and jump straight to a generation. Each entry has three tabs: info, base stats and the evolution line.

Progress comes from real saves. Drop your Gen 3 saves (FireRed/LeafGreen, Ruby/Sapphire/Emerald) in `/saves` on the SD card and the board parses them itself to see what you've seen and caught. Species you haven't met yet stay a mystery: the name becomes `???` and the stats tabs are locked.

The interface is deliberately calm. There's no scrolling, no animation, and one tap means one redraw. That isn't only taste: a full screen is about 150 KB in RGB565 and takes around 30 ms of SPI just to push, and the panel has no vsync, so animations tear. Paging with ◀ ▶ buttons is faster and looks better on this hardware. The theme is dark because the CYD's TN panel washes out light backgrounds as soon as you look from an angle. It ships in English and Portuguese.

## Fitting it in with no PSRAM

The board has no PSRAM, and the heap is split into regions whose largest contiguous block at boot is about 47 KB. Connecting to Wi-Fi takes another ~70 KB and fragments what's left. Most of the engineering went into making the catalog, the sprites and Wi-Fi live together:

- **Memory is reserved up front, in a fixed order.** The display buffer and the species names come out of a single early allocation, and sprites get a fixed 46 KB area split into ten slots. Every screen's worst case fits exactly: the grid's ten 48 px sprites, the entry's 96 px sprite plus three 64 px ones for the evolution line, the showcase's 96 px card. Before this, the last sprites of the grid failed to load whenever Wi-Fi was on.
- **Only one screen exists at a time.** The previous screen is destroyed before the next one is built, and each screen frees its own sprites and timers when it's deleted.
- **Big files are read in chunks.** Species data and sprites are packed into a few files on a FAT32 card and streamed from it. The SD card is mounted before Wi-Fi so its buffers get the room they need.
- **Stack size is measured, not guessed.** The deepest call chain (parsing a Gen 3 save under LVGL) was measured with `-fstack-usage`, which let the loop task drop from 64 KB to 32 KB and give the rest back to Wi-Fi.

The data itself is generated by a Python script that downloads species and sprites from PokeAPI on your machine, so the repository has no game data in it.

## Showcase mode

Left alone, CYDex becomes a trading card that rotates through your collection, every 30 seconds up to every 15 minutes. It shuffles without repeating, dims itself between sunset and sunrise (computed on the board from your city and the NTP clock), and moves a pixel or two on every change so the panel doesn't burn in.

## Notices from anything on the network

With Wi-Fi on, the board answers at `http://cydex.local`, and a small HTTP API lets any script, CI job or smart home hub put a message on the screen:

```sh
curl -4 http://cydex.local/notices \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $CYDEX_KEY" \
  -d '{"title":"Build passed","subtitle":"GitHub Actions","type":"success"}'
```

Notices have colors, icons, priorities and timeouts. Sending the same `id` again updates the notice instead of adding a new one, so one build or one chat keeps a single entry. Outside the showcase the most important notice shows up as a banner over the current screen. On the showcase they stack as speech bubbles next to the sprite.

The integration I use most is **Claude Code**. The repo ships hooks that tell the display when Claude needs a permission, is waiting for me or has finished, and clear the notice once I'm back at the terminal. Only the status goes over the network. Prompts never leave the computer.

## A desktop companion

There's also a small tray app for macOS, Windows and Linux, built with Tauri 2 (Rust + TypeScript). It finds the CYD on the network by itself, turns the display off when the computer sleeps and back on when it wakes, uploads save files over Wi-Fi by drag and drop, shows progress per generation and open notices, and sends your city so the board knows when the sun sets.

## How it's built

The firmware is C++ on PlatformIO and Arduino, with the UI in LVGL 8 using custom Montserrat fonts and icons generated from SVGs. All the logic that doesn't depend on the board (the save parser, search, paging, notices, translations) lives in a separate library and runs as native unit tests on the computer. One test fails if a translation is missing, if its `%` placeholders differ from the reference, or if it uses a character the fonts have no glyph for.

Every screen was designed first on a canvas at the display's real size, 320×240, and the firmware follows it: when the UI changes, the canvas changes first. Screenshots in the README are real captures, pulled from the board's framebuffer over USB by a serial command.

## Try it

You'll need an ESP32-2432S028 (the one without the "R"), a microSD card and, if you want tap sounds, a small speaker.

```sh
python3 tools/build_sd.py --out build/sd   # build the SD card contents
pio run -e cyd -t upload                   # flash the firmware
```

Copy `build/sd/` to the card, put your saves in `/saves` and power it up. The [README](https://github.com/hitaloose/cydex#readme) has more screenshots, and the desktop app is on the [releases](https://github.com/hitaloose/cydex/releases) page. Next on the list: saves from other generations, save backups through the desktop app, and trading between two boards over ESP-NOW.
